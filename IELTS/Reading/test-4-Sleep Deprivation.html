Sleep Deprivation

Reading Passage: The following passage discusses the effects of sleep deprivation on health, cognition, and society.
Paragraph A
Most people need between seven and nine hours of sleep per night to function optimally. According to psychologists and psychiatrists, Americans should be getting eight or more hours of sleep each night. However, research shows that many adults regularly get less than this recommended amount. David Dinges, a sleep researcher at the University of Pennsylvania, has conducted experiments where subjects stay in bed for at least 14 hours a day. He found that most people sleep about 12 hours a day for several days when given the opportunity, suggesting they are paying back a "sleep debt."
Paragraph B
Sleep deprivation has significant effects on cognitive functioning. People who are sleep-deprived show impairments in memory, diminished ability to make decisions, and dramatic lapses in attention. These deficits can be as severe as those caused by alcohol intoxication. In one study, participants who were awake for 17-19 hours performed worse on cognitive tests than those with a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%.
Paragraph C
The consequences of sleep deprivation extend beyond individual health. Stanley Coren, a psychologist at the University of British Columbia, studied the effects of daylight saving time on traffic accidents. He found that there was a 17% increase in traffic deaths on the Monday following the spring time change, when people lose one hour of sleep. This suggests that even small amounts of sleep loss can have serious real-world consequences.
Paragraph D
The economic impact of sleep deprivation is substantial. According to the National Sleep Foundation, sleeplessness costs the U.S. economy approximately $18 billion annually in lost worker productivity. Sleep-deprived workers are more likely to make errors, take more sick days, and have lower overall job performance.
Paragraph E
Fortunately, the treatment for chronic sleep deprivation is simple, free, and has no negative side effects: get more sleep. William Dement, founder of the Stanford Sleep Disorders Clinic, estimates that millions of Americans are living a less than optimal life because of accumulated sleep debt. He argues that addressing this issue could significantly improve public health and safety.
Paragraph F
Research also shows that napping can help reduce sleep debt and improve cognitive functioning. Even short naps of 20-30 minutes can provide benefits in alertness and performance. However, napping is not a complete substitute for adequate nighttime sleep, and should be used as a supplement rather than a replacement.
