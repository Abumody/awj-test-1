The Stop-Motion Legend

A Big Gorilla Started It All
In Greek mythology, Jason sails to find the Golden Fleece. In Jason and the Argonauts (1963), King Aeetes throws Hydra's teeth onto the ground, and seven armed skeletons rise to fight. This famous sequence was created by special effects master Ray Harryhausen, who inspired later films like Jurassic Park and The Lord of the Rings.

Harryhausen used stop-motion animation: one-foot-tall jointed skeletons, photographed frame by frame. He adjusted their bodies slightly for each shot. The skeleton battle took 4½ months to film, but the result was far more realistic than cartoons. Born in Los Angeles in 1920, Harryhausen fell in love with effects after seeing King Kong (1933) at age 13. He made his first creature from his mother's fur coat and a borrowed camera. He studied drawing, ceramics, and sculpture to master the craft.

In the 1940s, he made Mother Goose Stories and was hired by Willis O'Brien for Mighty Joe Young (1949), which won an Oscar. During the 1950s, he created sci-fi classics: The Beast from 20,000 Fathoms (dinosaur attacks New York), It Came from Beneath the Sea (octopus destroys Golden Gate Bridge), and 20 Million Miles to Earth. From 1958 to 1981, he turned to fantasy adventures: The Seventh Voyage of Sinbad (Cyclops, two-headed bird), Jason and the Argonauts (hydra, skeletons), and Clash of the Titans (Medusa, Kraken).

Though never nominated for a competitive Oscar, Harryhausen received a lifetime achievement Oscar in 1992. Tom Hanks joked that Jason and the Argonauts is greater than Citizen Kane. Today, CGI has replaced stop-motion, but directors like Peter Jackson ( Lord of the Rings, King Kong ) credit Harryhausen as their inspiration. His films are known as "Ray Harryhausen films," defined by his kinetic magic and imagination.

Hydra: serpent-like beast with many heads. Stop-motion: frame-by-frame model animation. Performance-capture: digital recording of actor movements.
